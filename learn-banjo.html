Beginner Banjo

Tenor banjo for complete beginners. Never played a note? Here’s where to start — seven steps from tuning up to your first session tunes, using free tools you can open right here. No sign-up needed.

A note from William

I teach tenor banjo and Irish trad, and this is the order I start a new adult student in. Take each step at your own pace — a week each is about right — and don’t rush past the slow bits. Ten or fifteen minutes a day does more than an hour on a Sunday.

You’ll need a tenor banjo tuned G D A E (the Irish tuning — the same notes as a fiddle or mandolin, an octave lower) and a plectrum. Some of the tools listen to you through the microphone, so a phone or laptop with a mic is ideal.

0 of 7 steps done
1

Tune up, and hear when you’re out

Week one · every time you pick it up

Everything sounds wrong on a banjo that’s out of tune, so this comes first, every time. The four strings, low to high, are G D A E. Use the tuner to get each one right, then train your ear to hear it for yourself.

  • Hold the plectrum between your thumb and the side of your first finger, with only a little of the tip showing. Keep it relaxed.
  • Pick down–up–down–up on one string, slowly and evenly, before you worry about anything else.
Tuner → In Tune? (banjo) → Out of Tune? (banjo) →
2

Your first scale: D major

Week one to two

D major is the key of more session tunes than any other, so it’s the scale to know first: open D string, then up across the A and E strings. In first position almost everything sits on the open strings and the first five frets.

  • A tenor banjo neck is long, so most trad players don’t use one finger per fret. Try your first finger on fret 2, middle finger on 3 or 4, and little finger on 5.
  • Play it slowly with the metronome on about 60, evenly, down–up with the pick.
D major on tenor banjo → Metronome →
3

Your first tune: The Britches Full of Stitches

Week two to four

A polka — short, bouncy, and sitting right inside that D major scale, which is why it’s the classic first tune. Open it in the Melody Trainer: it shows the tune on the stave and waits for you to play each note on your banjo before it moves on, so you can go as slowly as you need.

The Britches Full of Stitches → Next: John Ryan’s Polka →
4

A slower tune: Sí Bheag Sí Mhór

Week four to six · a waltz

Now something with more room in it. Sí Bheag Sí Mhór (“little fairy hill, big fairy hill”) is said to be the first tune the harper Turlough O’Carolan wrote, three hundred years ago, and it’s still played at nearly every session. It’s in D, so your scale already has every note.

It’s a waltz: three beats in every bar instead of the polka’s two. Count it ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three, leaning on the first beat of each bar, and let it sway rather than bounce. Waltzes are played slowly and smoothly, which gives you time to make each note ring clearly and to keep the pick moving evenly — the habits that make the faster tunes work later.

  • Set the metronome to 3 beats per bar, so the accent falls on each ONE, at a slow tempo (around 80).
Sí Bheag Sí Mhór → Metronome →
5

Find your way round the neck

Week six to seven

You’ve been playing notes by shape; now learn their names, so you can find any note without thinking. Start with D major only, then open it up to every note up to fret 5.

What Note? in D major → What Note? (every note) → Every note on the neck →
6

A second key: G major, and a jig

Week seven to nine

G major is the other big session key — the same idea as D, starting from the open G string. Then try your first jig: jigs go in threes (“di-dle-dy, di-dle-dy”), a new feel after the polkas.

G major on tenor banjo → The Kesh (jig) → The Stack of Barley (barndance) →
7

Keep your ears growing

From here on

Trad is learned by ear as much as off the page — at a session, someone plays a phrase and you play it back. Practise exactly that: hear a few notes, then find them on your banjo. A few minutes a day makes picking up tunes far easier. When D and G feel at home, the minor keys come next — E minor and A minor turn up in a great many reels.
